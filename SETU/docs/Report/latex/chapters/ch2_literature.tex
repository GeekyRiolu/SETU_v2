\chapter{Literature Survey}
\label{ch:literature}

This chapter surveys the research that \setu{} builds on and reacts against.
The survey has three purposes. First, it establishes what is technically
available for compressing a translation model to edge size, for training a
compact student from a strong teacher, and for covering many Indian languages at
once. Second, it compares those available technologies so that the choices made
in Chapter~\ref{ch:design} can be justified rather than asserted. Third, it
isolates the specific capability that none of the surveyed systems provides:
offline, on-device, multi-directional Indic translation within 200\,MB, which
becomes the problem statement of this project.

Twenty papers published from 2019 onward are reviewed, ordered by year of
publication. They fall into five groups: compact and mobile neural machine
translation, knowledge distillation, preference optimisation and alignment,
multilingual and Indic-specific translation, and offline or edge deployment.

\section{Introduction to the Survey}
\label{sec:litintro}

Neural machine translation converged on the Transformer architecture, and with
it on a scaling regime in which quality is bought with parameters and data.
That regime produced IndicTrans2, which translates all twenty-two scheduled
Indian languages at high quality but requires GPU-class hardware. The research
directions that matter for this project are the ones that ask what can be
recovered after the parameters are taken away again.

Three lines of work answer that question. Compression research (pruning,
quantisation, shallow architectures) reduces a trained model to a size a
device can hold. Distillation research transfers behaviour from a large teacher
to a small student, either by matching output distributions or by training the
student on the teacher's decoded output. Alignment research, developed for large
language models, replaces a single reference target with a preference signal
over several candidate outputs. The central question that this project settles
empirically is which of the latter two gives a very small translation student
the better training signal when the available parallel data is web-mined and
noisy.

A fourth line, multilingual translation, matters for coverage. Shared
multilingual models exploit transfer between related languages, which is
valuable for the Indian language family, but they also introduce interference
between directions and pivoting error when no direct model exists.

\section{Summary of Papers Surveyed}
\label{sec:papers}

Each entry below gives the paper, its authors and where it appeared, followed
by what it contributes and where it falls short for this project's purposes.

\paper{1}{DistilBERT, a distilled version of BERT: smaller, faster, cheaper and lighter}{V. Sanh, L. Debut, J. Chaumond, and T. Wolf}{\textit{arXiv preprint} arXiv:1910.01108, 2019}
Introduces a general-purpose language model distilled from BERT during
pre-training rather than fine-tuning, using a triple objective that combines
masked language modelling with a distillation loss over the teacher's output
distribution and a cosine loss that aligns the two hidden spaces. The student
halves the layer count yet retains about 97\,\% of its teacher's
language-understanding performance, which established that a compact student
trained on a teacher's soft targets can approach the teacher at a fraction of
its size. \setu{} takes two things from this work: the evidence that aggressive
layer reduction is survivable, and the principle that the training signal
should come from the teacher rather than from the raw corpus.
\textbf{Drawback:} the result is demonstrated for encoders on classification
tasks, not for autoregressive sequence generation, where errors compound across
decoding steps; and a measurable accuracy loss remains on the more complex
tasks, so the technique transfers to translation only as a hypothesis to be
tested, which is what this project does.

\paper{2}{Neural Machine Translation on Mobile Devices}{B. Li, Z. Wang, H. Liu, Y. Jiang, Q. Du, T. Xiao, H. Wang, and J. Zhu}{\textit{IEEE}, 2020}
Addresses the engineering problem of running neural machine translation on
phones and embedded hardware, combining model compression with runtime
optimisations to cut both memory footprint and inference latency far enough for
real-time on-device translation. It is the clearest demonstration that the
deployment target this project assumes is reachable, and it establishes the
measurement discipline that matters on edge hardware: report latency and
artifact size alongside quality rather than quality alone.
\textbf{Drawback:} the reduction techniques degrade translation quality, and
the degradation concentrates on long and syntactically complex sentences, which
are exactly the inputs where a user most needs the translation to be reliable.
The paper also does not address Indic morphology or the multi-script problem
that dominates Indian-language translation.

\paper{3}{SMaLL-100: Introducing Shallow Multilingual Machine Translation for Low-Resource Languages}{A. Mohammadshahi, V. Nikoulina, A. B\'{e}rard, C. Brun, J. Henderson, and L. Besacier}{\textit{Springer}, 2020}
Distils the 12-billion-parameter M2M-100 multilingual translator into a
shallow model that keeps wide language coverage while using substantially less
memory and running considerably faster. It is direct evidence that a
multilingual translation model can be compressed without abandoning
low-resource directions, and that the resulting student remains usable for the
languages that have the least data, which is the population this project
serves. \textbf{Drawback:} quality still trails the large model it compresses,
and the gap is widest on high-resource pairs where the extra capacity of the
teacher genuinely buys something. The compressed model is also still far larger
than a 200\,MB per-direction budget allows, so the work sets a direction rather
than a deployable artifact.

\paper{4}{The Tatoeba Translation Challenge -- Realistic Data Sets for Low Resource and Multilingual MT}{J. Tiedemann}{\textit{Proceedings of the Sixth Conference on Machine Translation (WMT)}, 2021}
Releases a translation benchmark spanning 555 languages assembled from the
Tatoeba collection, deliberately constructed so that evaluation reflects
realistic rather than curated conditions, and enabling systematic comparison
across low-resource, zero-shot and few-shot settings. For this project its
value is methodological: it makes the case that a system claiming broad
language coverage must be measured on held-out data of the kind it will
actually meet, not on the cleanest slice available.
\textbf{Drawback:} the data is noisy and inconsistent in quality from one
language to the next, so results are not comparable across directions without
care; and exploiting the full breadth of the benchmark still requires
large-capacity models, which puts it out of reach of a compact on-device
student.

\paper{5}{Shallow-to-Deep Training for Neural Machine Translation}{B. Li, Z. Wang, H. Liu, Y. Jiang, Q. Du, T. Xiao, H. Wang, and J. Zhu}{\textit{Proceedings of the 59th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL)}, 2021}
Proposes training deep Transformer translation models incrementally,
beginning with a shallow stack and adding layers progressively so that each new
block starts from an already-converged state. The schedule improves convergence
speed and keeps training stable at depths where a from-scratch run would
diverge, and it supplies a useful observation for this project: the stability of
a translation model is as much a property of the training schedule as of the
architecture, a point this project met directly in its own learning-rate
collapses. \textbf{Drawback:} the final models remain computationally heavy, so
the method improves how a large model is trained rather than producing a small
one, and the staged schedule adds engineering complexity that is hard to
justify for a 52-million-parameter student.

\paper{6}{Nearest Neighbor Knowledge Distillation for Neural Machine Translation}{Z. Yang, R. Sun, and X. Wan}{\textit{IEEE}, 2022}
Augments knowledge distillation for translation with $k$-nearest-neighbour
retrieval over the training set, so that the student receives supervision not
only from the teacher's output but also from similar examples retrieved at
training time. The additional signal improves quality noticeably in
low-resource conditions, which is the regime that matters for most Indian
languages, and the idea of enriching a thin training signal with retrieved
neighbours directly informed this project's preference-generation stage.
\textbf{Drawback:} the retrieval datastore is memory-intensive and slows both
training and inference, and an inference-time datastore is disqualifying for
edge deployment, where the whole artifact must fit inside roughly 200\,MB and
answer in under half a second.

\paper{7}{Speech-to-Speech Translation Improvements with Self-Supervised Learning}{C. Xu, R. Ye, Q. Dong, C. Zhao, T. Ko, M. Wang, T. Xiao, and J. Zhu}{\textit{IEEE}, 2022}
Combines self-supervised pre-training, multitask learning and synthetic
data generation to obtain large BLEU gains in speech-to-speech translation,
with the biggest improvements on low-resource pairs where labelled speech is
scarce. It demonstrates how far synthetic and self-supervised signal can
substitute for missing supervision, a question that bears directly on whether
teacher-generated targets can replace noisy human references.
\textbf{Drawback:} the approach depends on large-scale pre-training and a
multi-stage pipeline whose compute and storage requirements make it impractical
to deploy on a device, and it addresses the speech modality, which is outside
the text-to-text scope of this project.

\paper{8}{Direct Speech Translation}{Y. Jia, Y. Ding, A. Bapna, C. Cherry, Y. Zhang, A. Conneau, and N. Morioka}{\textit{Springer}, 2023}
Surveys end-to-end speech translation and characterises the three
obstacles that dominate the field: scarcity of paired speech-translation data,
the latency cost of streaming inference, and systematic bias introduced when
synthetic or pivoted data stands in for real supervision. The survey is useful
here as a map of what an offline speech front-end over a text engine would have
to overcome, and it is the basis for treating speech as future work rather than
scope. \textbf{Drawback:} it is a survey rather than a system, offering no
concrete model and no experimental validation, so it frames problems without
supplying a solution that could be adopted or measured.

\paper{9}{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}{R. Rafailov, A. Sharma, E. Mitchell, C. D. Manning, S. Ermon, and C. Finn}{\textit{Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS)}, vol.~36, 2023}
Reformulates preference alignment as a simple classification loss over
pairs of preferred and dispreferred responses, showing that the optimal policy
of a reward model has a closed form that lets the reward model and the
reinforcement-learning loop of RLHF be removed entirely. The result is a
training procedure that is markedly more stable and cheaper to run than PPO
while reaching comparable alignment quality. This paper is the origin of the
project's founding hypothesis: if preference learning improves large models so
cleanly, ranked teacher hypotheses might be a better training signal for a tiny
translation student than the references themselves.
\textbf{Drawback:} its effectiveness depends heavily on the quality and
diversity of the preference data, and it studies alignment of large language
models rather than distillation into small ones, so nothing in it guarantees
the method survives a hundredfold reduction in student capacity. This project
tested that transfer under controlled conditions and found it does not.

\paper{10}{All-in-One Knowledge Distillation Framework for Neural Machine Translation}{Z. Miao, W. Zhang, J. Su, X. Li, J. Luan, Y. Chen, B. Wang, and M. Zhang}{\textit{Proceedings of the 2023 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing (EMNLP)}, 2023}
Presents a framework that distils a single teacher into several student
models at once, sharing the teacher's forward computation across students
instead of repeating it, which cuts training cost substantially and makes
multilingual distillation at scale practical. The mechanism, including
temperature-sampled cross-lingual balancing that stops high-resource directions
from dominating the batch, is implemented and tested in this project as the
route to consolidating per-direction students into one multilingual model.
\textbf{Drawback:} joint optimisation across students creates gradient
conflicts between directions, and the directions that lose those conflicts are
the low-resource ones, which is precisely the opposite of what a system for
Indian languages needs; per-direction training avoids the interference at the
cost of storage.

\paper{11}{Continual Knowledge Distillation for Neural Machine Translation}{Y. Zhang, P. Li, M. Sun, and Y. Liu}{\textit{Proceedings of the 61st Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL)}, 2023}
Addresses the problem of adding a new language to an existing
multilingual translation model without retraining from scratch and without
degrading the languages already covered, using distillation from the model's
own earlier state to preserve prior capability. For a system that claims
extensibility this is the mechanism that makes the claim credible: a new
language should be a configuration and data change rather than a rewrite, and
the related Elastic Weight Consolidation machinery in this project serves the
same purpose. \textbf{Drawback:} training complexity grows with each task
added, and the stability-plasticity balance, how strongly to anchor old
parameters against how freely to fit the new language, requires careful tuning
that the paper does not reduce to a rule.

\paper{12}{IndicTrans2: Towards High-Quality and Accessible Machine Translation Models for All 22 Scheduled Indian Languages}{J. Gala, P. A. Chitale, A. K. Raghavan, V. Gumma, S. Doddapaneni, A. Kumar M, and J. A. Nawale}{\textit{Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR)}, 2023}
Delivers open translation models covering all twenty-two scheduled
Indian languages in both directions, together with the Bharat Parallel Corpus
Collection used to train them and the IN22 benchmark used to evaluate them, and
it is the current quality reference point for Indic machine translation. It
supplies this project with its teacher, its script-qualified FLORES language
codes, and the quality target against which every student is measured: the
project gates on the ratio of student BLEU to this teacher's BLEU rather than
on an absolute score. \textbf{Drawback:} the flagship models hold roughly a
billion parameters and need GPU-class compute with multiple gigabytes of
resident memory, which rules out offline edge deployment entirely; the gated
distribution of the official checkpoints and of BPCC is a further practical
obstacle, and it is why this project distils from the ungated distilled-200M
variant and reports the lower ceiling that follows.

\paper{13}{Multilingual Indian Language Neural Machine Translation System Using mT5 Transformer}{A. Jha, H. Y. Patil, S. K. Jindal, and S. M. N. Islam}{\textit{IEEE}, 2023}
Fine-tunes the mT5 multilingual encoder-decoder for Indian-language
translation and reports strong BLEU on the directions it covers, confirming
that a large pre-trained multilingual backbone transfers usefully to Indic data
and that shared representations help the lower-resource languages in the set.
It is evidence that multilingual transfer is real for this language family,
which is what makes an English-pivot architecture plausible.
\textbf{Drawback:} the evaluation extends to only a handful of languages rather
than the full Eighth Schedule, and the system inherits mT5's size, so it scales
neither to all twenty-two languages nor to on-device inference; the paper
reports quality without reporting the memory or latency those numbers cost.

\paper{14}{SETU-DCU Submissions to IWSLT 2024 Low-Resource Speech-to-Text Translation Tasks}{M. Zafar, A. Castaldo, N. Gajakos, P. Nayak, R. Haque, and A. Way}{\textit{Proceedings of the 21st International Conference on Spoken Language Translation (IWSLT)}, 2024}
Reports submissions to the IWSLT 2024 low-resource speech translation
task and finds that cascaded pipelines, automatic speech recognition followed
by text translation, still outperform end-to-end speech translation when
labelled speech data is genuinely scarce, with competitive BLEU across the
tasks entered. The finding matters here because it means an offline speech
front-end could be built over this project's existing text engine rather than
requiring a separate end-to-end model, which is why speech is listed as
reachable future work. \textbf{Drawback:} cascading compounds recognition
errors into translation errors, so the pipeline's quality is bounded by the
weaker of two systems, and the end-to-end alternatives the paper compares
against remain too data-starved to settle the question.

\paper{15}{Direct Preference Optimization for Neural Machine Translation with Minimum Bayes Risk Decoding}{G. Yang, J. Chen, W. Lin, and B. Byrne}{\textit{Proceedings of the 2024 Conference of the North American Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics (NAACL)}, 2024}
Applies Direct Preference Optimization to neural machine translation,
constructing the preference pairs with Minimum Bayes Risk decoding so that the
preferred candidate is the one closest to the consensus of a sampled set rather
than one chosen by a separate reward model. The result shows that preference
learning can improve translation quality with comparatively little preference
data, and it is the closest published precedent for the hypothesis this project
set out to test. \textbf{Drawback:} the evaluation does not extend to extremely
low-resource languages, where reference noise and data scarcity change the
problem, and the models studied are far larger than an edge-deployable student,
so the result does not establish that the same gain survives at 52 million
parameters.

\paper{16}{Multilingual NMT System for English to Low-Resource Indic Languages -- Assamese and Bengali}{K. Kashyap and S. K. Sarma}{\textit{IEEE}, 2024}
Builds a one-to-many multilingual system translating English into
Assamese and Bengali, improving quality on both low-resource targets through
shared multilingual representations and back-translated synthetic data that
compensates for the thin parallel corpora available. It is a useful existence
proof that the lower-resource end of the Eighth Schedule is tractable with
careful data engineering rather than more parameters.
\textbf{Drawback:} back-translated data introduces noise of its own, and
training on it risks teaching the model the artefacts of the reverse system;
coverage is also limited to two languages in a single direction, which leaves
the general multilingual and Indic-to-Indic problems unaddressed.

\paper{17}{Multilingual Neural Machine Translation System for Indic to Indic Languages}{S. B. Das, D. Panda, T. K. Mishra, B. K. Patra, and A. Ekbal}{\textit{ScienceDirect}, 2024}
Tackles translation directly between Indian languages rather than
through English, using shared multilingual models and pivoting to cover pairs
for which no direct parallel corpus exists. It is one of the few papers to take
the Indic-to-Indic problem seriously, a direction largely ignored by
English-centric systems and the one that matters most for communication within
India. The pivoting strategy it describes is the same one this project adopts
for the pairs it cannot train directly. \textbf{Drawback:} error propagates
through the pivot language, so accuracy degrades measurably relative to a
directly trained model, and the paper does not quantify how much of the loss is
attributable to the second hop, which leaves the cost of pivoting an open
question that this project also records as unmeasured.

\paper{18}{LLM-based AI-Powered Offline Portable Transcriptor (OPT) -- SURYA-TAC: A Tactical Speech-to-Speech Translation System}{P. S. Bohra and K. Berwal}{\textit{IEEE}, 2024}
Proposes an offline, edge-deployed speech-to-speech translation system
intended for tactical use in environments with no connectivity, and is the
closest published statement of the motivation behind this project: translation
that must work where the network does not, on hardware the user already
carries, without sending anything to a server. It establishes the operational
case for offline translation independently of the machine-learning argument.
\textbf{Drawback:} the work is largely conceptual, presented without real-world
validation or measured quality, latency and size figures, and it is constrained
by the same data scarcity for the target languages that it sets out to solve,
so it names the problem more convincingly than it solves it.

\paper{19}{Latency-Aware Simultaneous Machine Translation Using Large Language Models}{B. Fu, M. Liao, K. Fan, C. Li, L. Zhang, Y. Chen, and X. Shi}{\textit{Springer}, 2024}
Introduces latency-aware token generation so that a large language model
can perform simultaneous translation, deciding when to emit output rather than
waiting for a complete input, and holds accuracy while bounding the delay. The
work is a reminder that latency is a first-class quality attribute in
translation and not an implementation detail, which is why this project gates
on a measured per-sentence budget alongside BLEU.
\textbf{Drawback:} the method is computationally expensive and premised on
models far too large for a handset, so its latency guarantees are relative to a
baseline that already assumes server-class hardware; nothing in it reduces the
cost of the model itself.

\paper{20}{Offline English-to-Hindi Statistical Machine Translation System}{S. Gangwar and I. Naim}{\textit{ScienceDirect}, 2024}
Implements genuinely offline English-to-Hindi translation with the Moses
statistical machine translation toolkit, demonstrating that offline operation
is achievable on ordinary hardware at low computational cost. It is the only
surveyed system that actually runs without a network, and it confirms the
practical demand this project addresses. \textbf{Drawback:} statistical machine
translation is substantially weaker than neural approaches, particularly on
long-range reordering and morphology, so the system trades quality for
offline operation rather than achieving both; and it covers a single direction
of a single language pair, leaving the multilingual problem untouched. The gap
between this paper's offline capability and IndicTrans2's quality is precisely
the gap this project sets out to close.



\subsection{Discussion of Key Papers}
\label{subsec:keypapers}

Four of the surveyed papers determined the shape of this project and merit
closer discussion.

\textbf{IndicTrans2} \cite{ref:indictrans2} is the teacher. Its contribution is
not only the models but the surrounding artifacts: the BPCC corpus and the IN22
benchmark, which together made Indic MT reproducible. \setu{} adopts its
script-qualified FLORES language codes (\texttt{hin\_Deva}, \texttt{eng\_Latn})
as internal identifiers so that student tokenizer tags remain stable as
languages are added. The project's quality target (80\,\% of teacher BLEU) is
defined relative to this system. A practical constraint arose here: the official
\texttt{ai4bharat} checkpoints and the BPCC dataset are access-gated on Hugging
Face, returning HTTP 401 without an authenticated account that has accepted the
terms. The project therefore uses the ungated rotary distilled-200M checkpoints
released by IndicTrans2 co-author Raj Dabre, and the ungated Samanantar corpus.
This lowers the achievable ceiling, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} quantifies by
how much.

\textbf{Direct Preference Optimization} \cite{ref:dpo} supplied the project's
original hypothesis. Its key insight (that the optimal policy under a
Bradley-Terry preference model has a closed form, so preference learning reduces
to a classification loss on preferred and dispreferred pairs) makes preference
training cheap enough to attempt on a student model. \setu{} implements DPO with
a frozen supervised-fine-tuning reference model, chrF-ranked teacher hypotheses
as preferred candidates, and $k$-nearest-neighbour retrieved translations as
additional negatives. The implementation works as specified: the margin accuracy
reaches 1.0, meaning the trained policy ranks every preferred candidate above
its dispreferred counterpart. It nonetheless fails to improve translation
quality over sequence-level distillation, for reasons developed in
Section~\ref{sec:proposedsolution} and Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\textbf{Sequence-level knowledge distillation}, introduced by Kim and Rush
\cite{ref:seqkd} and instantiated in this project's baseline, replaces the human
reference target with the teacher's own one-best decoded output. It is the
approach that won. The mechanism is best understood as denoising: the teacher's
output is internally consistent and drawn from a single distribution, whereas
web-mined references are inconsistent in register, alignment quality and
fidelity. A 52-million-parameter student has no spare capacity to average over
that inconsistency.

\textbf{DistilBERT} \cite{ref:distilbert} and \textbf{SMaLL-100}
\cite{ref:small100} between them establish the size-quality frontier that this
project operates on. DistilBERT shows that roughly 97\,\% of a large model's
performance survives halving its depth; SMaLL-100 shows the same for
multilingual translation specifically. Both suggest that a compact student can
retain most of a teacher's ability, which is what the 0.80 ratio target
formalises.

\section{Comparative Study of Available Technologies}
\label{sec:comparative}

This section compares the candidate technologies for each design decision the
project faced, and records the choice made together with its justification.

Each design decision below lists the alternatives that were considered, the
trade-off between them, and the choice the project made with its reason.

\decision{Teacher model}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} IndicTrans2 1B; IndicTrans2 distilled-200M; mT5-based Indic NMT; NLLB-200. \emph{Trade-off:} The 1B flagship gives the highest ceiling but is gated and needs roughly 5\,GB for weights alone; mT5 covers fewer Indic languages. \textbf{Ungated rotary IndicTrans2 distilled-200M.} Accessible without an authenticated account, fits available memory, and covers all 22 scheduled languages. The lower ceiling is reported rather than hidden

\decision{Training signal for the student}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} Human references (plain SFT); teacher one-best (\seqkd{}); chrF-ranked preference pairs with DPO; \seqkd{} followed by DPO. \emph{Trade-off:} References are abundant but noisy; teacher output is clean but caps the student at teacher quality; preference pairs add ranking information but need a reliable ranker. \textbf{\seqkd{} on teacher one-best}, selected by the controlled experiment in Section~\ref{sec:proposedsolution}: 17.09 BLEU against 11.10 for reference-trained DPO at matched size

\decision{Student architecture}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} Marian encoder--decoder; distilled Transformer; shallow multilingual (SMaLL-100 style); decoder-only LM. \emph{Trade-off:} Encoder--decoder remains the strongest inductive bias for translation; decoder-only needs far more parameters for equivalent quality. \textbf{Marian-style Transformer, 6 encoder and 6 decoder layers, $d=512$, 52.6\,M parameters.} Quantises to 103.9\,MB, comfortably inside the 200\,MB budget

\decision{Vocabulary}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} Shared 32{,}000-token SentencePiece; 16{,}000-token SentencePiece; byte-level BPE. \emph{Trade-off:} A larger vocabulary shortens sequences but leaves rare tokens undertrained on a corpus of this size. \textbf{16{,}000-token SentencePiece.} A 32{,}000-token vocabulary was measured to produce degenerate decoding on this data volume (Section~\ref{sec:runlog})

\decision{Multilingual strategy}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} One model per direction; one shared multilingual model; shared model with All-in-One KD. \emph{Trade-off:} Per-direction models avoid interference and permit independent quality gates; a shared model saves storage but suffers low-resource interference \cite{ref:aiokd}. \textbf{One student per direction}, with the All-in-One KD mechanism implemented and tested for future consolidation

\decision{Indic-to-Indic coverage}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} Train $n(n-1)$ direct models; pivot through English; use a gated Indic-to-Indic teacher. \emph{Trade-off:} Direct models scale quadratically at 104\,MB each; pivoting doubles latency and can compound errors; the Indic-to-Indic teacher is gated. \textbf{English pivot.} $2n$ models serve $n^2$ directions; measured two-hop latency remains around 50--65\,ms

\decision{Language expansion}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} Full retraining; All-in-One KD; Elastic Weight Consolidation \cite{ref:continualkd}. \emph{Trade-off:} Retraining is prohibitive; EWC protects old languages via a Fisher-weighted penalty. \textbf{EWC implemented and validated} as the incremental path; per-direction training used for Phase~1

\decision{Quantisation and runtime}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} PyTorch on-device; TensorFlow Lite; ONNX Runtime with INT8/INT4; GGML. \emph{Trade-off:} ONNX Runtime has mature CPU kernels, a small footprint and wide platform support; its CPU provider lacks a true 4-bit kernel. \textbf{ONNX Runtime with progressive INT8 then INT4 export via Optimum.} 411\,MB FP32 compresses to 103.9\,MB

\decision{Evaluation metric}
\emph{Alternatives considered:} BLEU; chrF/chrF++; COMET; human evaluation. \emph{Trade-off:} BLEU is standard but penalises agglutinative languages; COMET correlates better with human judgement but needs a neural model at evaluation time. \textbf{BLEU and chrF via sacreBLEU}, reported as a student/teacher ratio; COMET implemented for future benchmarking


\subsection{Why These Technologies Were Selected}
\label{subsec:why}

The selection logic follows from the four objectives in
Section~\ref{sec:objectives} treated as hard constraints rather than
aspirations.

The 200\,MB size budget eliminates every alternative that keeps a teacher-sized
model on the device, and therefore mandates distillation into a student of
roughly 50 million parameters. That parameter budget in turn eliminates
retrieval-augmented approaches such as nearest-neighbour distillation
\cite{ref:knnkd}, whose datastore alone would exceed the budget.

The 500\,ms latency budget on CPU eliminates beam search at deployment (the
deployed engine decodes greedily, with beam search reserved for offline
evaluation) and eliminates any method requiring a retrieval lookup per token.

The offline requirement eliminates any runtime that fetches weights, tokenizer
artifacts or telemetry, which is why the tokenizer is a local SentencePiece
model rather than a Hugging Face identifier resolved at load time.

The quality target, finally, is what forced the empirical comparison. Both
sequence-level distillation and preference distillation were plausible routes to
80\,\% of teacher BLEU, and the literature did not settle the question for
students this small on data this noisy. The project therefore built both and
measured them.

\section{Drawbacks of the Existing System}
\label{sec:drawbacks}

The survey exposes five drawbacks that, taken together, leave the offline Indic
translation problem unsolved.

\textbf{Cloud dependency.} Almost every high-quality MT system in production
requires an active network connection. IndicTrans2 \cite{ref:indictrans2}, mT5
based systems \cite{ref:mt5indic} and LLM-based simultaneous translators
\cite{ref:latency} all assume server-side execution. In regions with intermittent
connectivity, which is where the language barrier is most acute, these
systems are unavailable exactly when needed. Cloud execution also means that
every translated sentence is transmitted to and processed by a third party.

\textbf{High compute requirements.} The models that achieve state-of-the-art
Indic quality hold hundreds of millions to billions of parameters and need
GPU-class memory. The IndicTrans2 flagship requires roughly 5\,GB for weights
alone. Mid-range handsets and ARM single-board computers cannot host them, and
the compression literature \cite{ref:mobilenmt, ref:small100} consistently reports
quality degradation as the price of shrinking them.

\textbf{Error propagation in cascaded pipelines.} Systems that chain automatic
speech recognition to translation \cite{ref:iwslt} compound recognition errors
into translation errors. The IWSLT 2024 results show cascades still outperforming
end-to-end models in low-resource settings, which means the compounding problem
is currently unavoidable in that design.

\textbf{Narrow language coverage.} Compact and specialised systems cover very few
languages. The mT5-based Indic system \cite{ref:mt5indic} evaluates on a handful;
the Assamese-Bengali system \cite{ref:assamese} covers two in one direction; the
offline SMT system \cite{ref:offlinesmt} covers a single direction of a single
pair. Systems with broad coverage are precisely the ones too large to deploy.

\textbf{Data scarcity for low-resource languages.} Languages such as Kashmiri,
Dogri, Konkani, Bodo and Santali lack parallel corpora at the scale neural
translation requires. Where corpora do exist they are frequently web-mined and
noisy, as the Tatoeba challenge \cite{ref:tatoeba} documents. This project
encountered the problem directly: only eleven of the twenty-two scheduled
languages had ungated data sufficient to train against.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

\begin{quote}
\itshape
There is a need for a lightweight, offline-capable multilingual translation
framework that delivers high-quality translation across the scheduled languages
of India on edge devices (retaining at least 80\,\% of a strong teacher's BLEU
within a 200\,MB artifact and under 500\,ms of CPU latency per sentence, with
zero network calls at inference) and that supports incremental addition of new
languages without catastrophic forgetting and without a quadratic growth in the
number of trained models.
\end{quote}

The overall problem decomposes into six sub-problems, each addressed by an
identifiable part of the system.

\begin{bullets}
\item \textbf{Corpus quality.} The available ungated parallel data (Samanantar) is web-mined, and contains misaligned pairs, length-ratio anomalies, wrong-script segments and duplicates. \emph{Sub-problem:} construct a cleaning pipeline that yields a corpus a 52-million-parameter student can learn from. Addressed by the Corpus Loader module (Section~\ref{subsec:mod1}).
\item \textbf{Choice of training signal.} It is not established which signal (human references, teacher one-best output, or preference-ranked teacher hypotheses) best trains a student an order of magnitude smaller than its teacher on noisy data. \emph{Sub-problem:} construct all three and compare them under matched conditions. Addressed by the Teacher Distiller and Trainer modules and reported in Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}.
\item \textbf{Training stability.} A Transformer trained from scratch at this scale is unstable: three distinct failure modes (empty output, unconditional decoding, and mid-training collapse) were encountered. \emph{Sub-problem:} identify and eliminate each. Addressed in Section~\ref{sec:runlog}.
\item \textbf{Deployment footprint.} A 52.6-million-parameter FP32 model occupies 411\,MB, over twice the size budget, and decodes too slowly in PyTorch on CPU. \emph{Sub-problem:} compress and accelerate without materially losing quality. Addressed by the Quantiser module (Section~\ref{subsec:mod4}).
\item \textbf{Indic-to-Indic coverage.} No ungated Indic-to-Indic teacher exists, and training direct models for every pair would require $n(n-1)$ models at 104\,MB each. \emph{Sub-problem:} serve every pair without quadratic growth. Addressed by the English-pivot router (Section~\ref{subsec:mod5}).
\item \textbf{Extensibility.} Adding a language must not require retraining everything or degrade the languages already supported. \emph{Sub-problem:} provide a mechanism for incremental expansion with measured retention. Addressed by the All-in-One KD and Elastic Weight Consolidation implementations (Section~\ref{subsec:mod8}).
\end{bullets}

\section{Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:proposedsolution}

\setu{} proposes a four-stage pipeline (distil, train, quantise, serve)
instantiated once per translation direction and bound together by a routing
layer that pivots through English.

\textbf{Stage 1: Teacher distillation.} The ungated IndicTrans2 distilled-200M
teacher decodes every source sentence in the cleaned corpus, and its one-best
output replaces the human reference as the training target. This is
sequence-level knowledge distillation in the sense of Kim and Rush
\cite{ref:seqkd}. Greedy decoding is used, which is four to five times faster than
beam search and produces targets of equivalent utility.

\textbf{Stage 2: Student training.} A 52.6-million-parameter Marian
encoder--decoder with a 16{,}000-token SentencePiece vocabulary is trained from
scratch on the distilled corpus with token-level cross-entropy, padding masked
out of the loss, linear learning-rate warmup and decay, label smoothing of 0.1,
dropout of 0.2 and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Every one of these components
was added in response to a measured failure, not adopted by default;
Section~\ref{sec:runlog} gives the history.

\textbf{Stage 3: Quantisation.} The trained student is exported to ONNX,
validated against ONNX Runtime, and quantised progressively to INT8 and then
INT4, with latency and size benchmarked at each stage. The result is a 103.9\,MB
artifact, a fourfold compression from 411\,MB.

\textbf{Stage 4: Offline serving.} An inference engine loads the quantised ONNX
model and a local SentencePiece tokenizer and decodes greedily. A router
resolves each request to a directly trained model where one exists, and
otherwise chains two students through English. One engine serves a REST API, a
command-line tool, an offline progressive web application and a browser client.

\subsection{The Deciding Experiment}
\label{subsec:deciding}

The project's founding hypothesis was H1: \emph{preference distillation produces
better compact students than sequence-level knowledge distillation}. It was
tested at matched student size, on identical data, with evaluation on real human
references held out from training, a design that is fair regardless of what
each system trained on. Figure~\ref{fig:h1} gives the outcome.

H1 is refuted. Sequence-level distillation beats preference distillation by
6.0 BLEU, an improvement of about 54\,\%, and DPO applied on top of a
reference-trained base adds 0.15 BLEU over that base, indistinguishable from
noise. The replacement hypothesis, H1$'$, is supported by every subsequent
result: \emph{reference noise, not the training objective, is what caps a
compact Indic student.} Teacher-distilled targets remove that noise, and the
student then continues to improve with data: the ratio rises from 0.607 at
100{,}000 sentences to 0.764 at 250{,}000 and 0.806 at 500{,}000, whereas
reference-trained students plateau near 0.66 regardless of data volume.

The DPO implementation is not defective, which is what makes the result
